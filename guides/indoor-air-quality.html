We spend most of our time indoors, and indoor air can carry more fine particles and chemicals than the air outside. The fixes are mostly cheap — ventilate at the right times, filter well, and stop pollution at the source.

The main indoor pollutants

  • Fine particles (PM2.5): from cooking (especially frying and searing), candles, incense, wood stoves, smoking and outdoor pollution seeping in.
  • VOCs (volatile organic compounds): from paints, adhesives, new furniture, air fresheners, some cleaning products and fragrances.
  • Nitrogen dioxide and carbon monoxide: from gas stoves and poorly vented combustion appliances.
  • Biological allergens: dust mites, pet dander, pollen and mould spores.
  • Radon: an invisible radioactive gas from the ground in some regions — only a test kit can tell you.

Ventilation: the cheapest upgrade

  1. Use the range hood every time you cook — ideally one that vents outdoors — and keep it on for 10–15 minutes afterwards. Cook on back burners when possible; hoods capture them better.
  2. Run bathroom fans during showers and for 20–30 minutes afterwards.
  3. Cross-ventilate when outdoor air is clean. Check our Live Clean Air Index or your local AQI: open windows on opposite sides for 10 minutes when it's good, and keep them closed during smog or wildfire smoke.
  4. Ventilate during and after cleaning or DIY — painting, varnishing and heavy cleaning all release VOCs.

Filtration and purifiers

HVAC filters: if you have forced-air heating or cooling, upgrade to a MERV 11–13 filter if your system can handle it (check the manual or ask an HVAC technician), and replace it every one to three months. Running the system fan more often increases filtration.

Portable HEPA purifiers: choose one sized for the room using its Clean Air Delivery Rate (CADR). A common rule of thumb is a smoke CADR (in cubic feet per minute) of at least two-thirds of the room area in square feet — for a 300 sq ft room, a CADR of 200+. Place it where you spend the most time (often the bedroom), keep doors closed for best effect, and replace filters on schedule. Avoid devices that intentionally generate ozone.

DIY box-fan filter: a box fan with a MERV 13 filter taped to the back is a well-studied, low-cost option for wildfire smoke events.

Cleaning habits that help — and hurt

  • Help: damp-dust instead of dry-dusting; vacuum with a sealed HEPA vacuum; wash bedding weekly; use doormats and a shoes-off policy; keep clutter (dust traps) down.
  • Hurt: heavy fragrance sprays and plug-ins, burning candles and incense indoors, mixing cleaning chemicals, using aerosols when a pump spray would do.

Fragrance-free and certified safer products reduce VOCs. Our green recipe library covers simple low-tox alternatives.

Humidity

Keep relative humidity between roughly 30% and 50%. Too high encourages dust mites and mould (see our mould guide); too low irritates airways. A hygrometer costs little and removes the guesswork; use a dehumidifier or humidifier accordingly, and clean humidifiers often so they don't spray microbes.

Do you need duct cleaning?

Routine duct cleaning isn't necessary for most homes. Consider it if you see visible mould growth inside ducts, evidence of pests, or excessive debris blowing from vents — or after a renovation that created lots of dust. Be wary of very low-priced "whole house" offers that turn into upsells; typical legitimate jobs run a few hundred dollars per system.

Quick checklist

  • ☐ Range hood on whenever cooking
  • ☐ MERV 11–13 filter, changed on schedule
  • ☐ HEPA purifier in the bedroom
  • ☐ Humidity 30–50%
  • ☐ CO alarm near sleeping areas
  • ☐ Radon test if you live in an affected region
  • ☐ Fragrance-free cleaners; no candles on bad-air days
