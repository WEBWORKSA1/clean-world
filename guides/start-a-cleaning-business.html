Cleaning is one of the lowest-cost businesses to start and one of the steadiest to run — demand doesn't disappear in a downturn, and recurring clients create predictable revenue. Here's a practical launch plan.

1. Choose your niche

  • Residential recurring: weekly or bi-weekly homes. Steady, relationship-driven.
  • Move-in / move-out and short-term rental turnovers: higher ticket, partners with realtors and property managers.
  • Commercial / janitorial: offices, clinics, retail. Contracts are larger and longer, but bidding is competitive and evening work is common.
  • Specialty: carpets, windows, post-construction, pressure washing — needs equipment, earns premium rates.

2. Startup costs

A solo residential cleaner can start with a few hundred to a couple of thousand dollars: supplies and equipment, a quality vacuum, business registration, general liability insurance, a simple website and booking tool, and transport. Specialty services need more capital (extractors, ladders, pressure washers).

3. Make it legal

  • Register your business and check local licence requirements.
  • Get general liability insurance; consider bonding (reassures clients) and workers' compensation once you hire.
  • Use written service agreements covering scope, pricing, cancellations and breakages.
  • Separate business banking from personal finances; track expenses from day one.

4. Price for profit

Common models are hourly ($30–$75 per cleaner-hour in 2026 US markets), flat-rate per visit (standard cleans commonly $120–$280) and per-square-foot for commercial work. Calculate your true hourly cost — labour, supplies, travel time, insurance, software, marketing — and aim for a healthy margin above it. Offer a recurring discount (10–25%) to lock in predictable revenue, and require a first-visit deep clean for new recurring clients.

5. Win your first clients

  • A clean, mobile-friendly site with clear prices and an online quote form
  • A Google Business Profile with real photos and reviews
  • Referral rewards for existing clients
  • Partnerships with realtors, property managers and short-term rental hosts
  • Lead networks — for example, the Clean.World pro network sends pre-qualified requests in your area

6. Systems before scale

Standardise checklists for every service, take before/after photos, and use scheduling and invoicing software. When you hire, train with your checklists, pay fairly and on time, and inspect early jobs. Retention — of clients and staff — is where cleaning businesses make or lose money.

Quick maths: 20 recurring clients at $160 every two weeks is about $6,900 per month in revenue. Every client you keep for a year is worth roughly $4,000 — which is why service quality and reliability beat discounting.
