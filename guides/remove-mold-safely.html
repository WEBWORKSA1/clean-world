Mould is a moisture problem first and a cleaning problem second. You can safely handle small patches yourself — but only if you also fix why it grew, and only if you know where DIY should stop.

Mould or mildew?

"Mildew" usually describes flat, powdery or downy growth that sits on the surface — common on shower grout, window frames and damp fabrics. "Mould" is a broader term and often appears fuzzy or slimy, in green, black, grey or white patches, sometimes growing into materials. Colour does not tell you how hazardous a mould is; any extensive growth should be treated seriously. A musty, earthy smell is a strong clue of hidden growth behind walls, under flooring or in HVAC systems.

The 10 square-foot rule

Public-health guidance in several countries, including the US EPA, suggests that a homeowner can generally clean up mould covering less than about 10 square feet (roughly 1 m² — a 3 ft × 3 ft patch) on hard surfaces. Larger areas, mould caused by sewage or contaminated water, mould inside HVAC systems, or any growth in the home of someone with asthma, allergies or a weakened immune system calls for professional advice.

Protective gear

  • An N95 / FFP2 respirator (a cloth mask is not enough)
  • Long rubber or nitrile gloves
  • Goggles without vent holes
  • Old clothes you can wash immediately afterwards

Keep children and pets out of the area, open windows, and run a fan pointing out of a window so spores are exhausted outdoors rather than circulated.

Cleaning hard, non-porous surfaces

  1. Dampen first. Lightly mist the patch with water. Dry scrubbing launches spores into the air.
  2. Scrub with detergent. Use warm water and a household detergent with a stiff brush or scrub pad. On tile, glass, sealed countertops, metal and bathtubs, this removes most surface growth.
  3. Optional disinfectant. For stubborn staining on non-porous surfaces, a product labelled for mould or a diluted bleach solution can be used — follow the label, ventilate, and never mix bleach with ammonia, vinegar or other cleaners. Bleach is not necessary to remove mould and does not penetrate porous materials.
  4. Rinse and dry fast. Wipe with clean water and dry completely within 24–48 hours using fans or a dehumidifier.
  5. Bag the waste. Seal used cloths and sponges in a plastic bag and dispose of them.

Soft and porous materials

Drywall, ceiling tiles, carpet padding, insulation and upholstered furniture absorb moisture and allow mould to grow inside them. Surface cleaning rarely removes it completely. Porous materials with significant growth usually need to be cut out and replaced. Washable fabrics with light mildew can often be saved: brush off outdoors, wash in the hottest water the label allows with oxygen bleach, and dry in sunlight.

Fix the moisture source

Mould returns unless you remove the water that feeds it. Typical causes and fixes:

  • Bathroom condensation: run the exhaust fan during and for 20–30 minutes after showers; squeegee walls; keep the door open afterwards.
  • Leaks: repair roof, plumbing and window leaks promptly; dry wet materials within 48 hours.
  • High humidity: aim for 30–50% indoor relative humidity. A cheap hygrometer tells you where you stand; a dehumidifier helps in basements.
  • Cold surfaces: condensation on exterior walls and windows can be reduced with better ventilation, insulation and keeping furniture a few centimetres off outside walls.
Renters: report persistent damp or mould to your landlord in writing with photos and dates. In many jurisdictions landlords must address the underlying cause.

When to call a professional

  • Growth larger than about 10 sq ft / 1 m², or in multiple rooms
  • Mould after flooding, sewage backup or a long-hidden leak
  • Musty smells with no visible source, or growth inside HVAC ducts
  • Anyone in the home with respiratory illness, immune suppression or a newborn
  • Mould that returns repeatedly after cleaning

Professional remediation typically ranges from about $500 for small, contained jobs to several thousand dollars for larger ones, depending on area, access and containment. Ask providers how they will contain the work area, what air filtration they use, and whether they recommend independent testing after the job.
